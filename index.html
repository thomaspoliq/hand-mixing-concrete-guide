Hand Mixing Concrete Guide

How to Mix Concrete by Hand in a Wheelbarrow

For fence posts, small pads and patch repairs, hand mixing in a wheelbarrow is often quicker than hiring a mixer. The method is simple, but the order you add the materials and the amount of water you use decide whether the finished concrete is strong or weak and crumbly.

The short answer: For a general purpose batch, blend 1 part cement, 2 parts sand and 4 parts coarse aggregate dry in the barrow until the colour is uniform, push the mix into a crater, then add clean water a little at a time, folding the dry material in from the edges. Stop as soon as the mix holds soft ridges and looks evenly damp. A soupy mix is a weak mix, so never drown a batch to make it easier to stir.

What you need before you start

You need a sturdy builder's wheelbarrow, a shovel, a bucket for measured water, plus cement, sharp sand and coarse aggregate (or bags of ready blended dry mix). A spare board gives you a clean, level mixing platform. Work close to where the concrete will be placed, because a loaded barrow is heavy to move.

Protective kit matters. Wet cement is strongly alkaline and can burn skin and damage eyes, so wear waterproof gloves, long sleeves and eye protection, and use a dust mask when tipping dry cement. Wash cement off your skin straight away, and never kneel in fresh concrete.

Mix ratios for common small jobs

Hand mixing is measured by volume, usually by the shovelful or the bucket. Keep the same measure for every ingredient so the proportions stay honest. Step by step: mixing a batch in the barrow

  1. Set up level. Park the barrow on your board so it cannot rock or tip while you stir.
  2. Add the aggregate and sand. Tip in the coarse aggregate first, then the sand, and turn them over together a few times.
  3. Add the cement. Pour the cement on top and keep turning the dry materials until the whole heap is one even colour with no streaks of grey or brown.
  4. Make a crater. Push the dry mix into a hollow in the middle, like a volcano.
  5. Add most of the water. Pour roughly two thirds of your expected water into the crater. Bagged mixes typically need around 2 to 3 litres per 20 kg bag; loose mixes vary, so treat that figure as a starting guide only.
  6. Fold, do not whisk. Pull dry material from the edges into the water with the shovel, turning the barrow load over itself. Chop and fold rather than beating air into it.
  7. Adjust in small amounts. Add the remaining water a splash at a time, mixing for a minute between additions, until the consistency is right (see the test below).
  8. Use it promptly. Place the batch straight away, then rinse the barrow and shovel before the residue hardens.

One comfortable barrow load is about one 25 kg bag of dry mix at a time, or roughly 0.03 to 0.05 cubic metres from loose materials. Bigger loads are hard to turn, and poorly turned concrete has dry pockets that never gain strength.

Water discipline and the consistency test

Water is the ingredient that ruins more hand mixed concrete than any other. The cement needs only enough water to react and to make the mix placeable. Every extra splash beyond that stays in the concrete as pores when it dries, which lowers strength and makes the surface dusty and prone to frost damage. If you remember one rule, make it this: add water gradually, and stop early.

Two simple checks tell you when a batch is right. First, drag the shovel through the mix: a good batch holds soft ridges and folds rather than flowing flat or tearing into dry crumbs. Second, take a gloved handful and squeeze gently. It should hold its shape like a snowball, leave your glove damp rather than dripping, and show no water running between your fingers. If it slumps into a puddle, it is too wet; if it will not bind at all, sprinkle in a little more water and fold again.

Worked example: bags for a small slab

Say you are laying a small equipment pad 1.2 m by 1.2 m at 100 mm thick. The volume is 1.2 x 1.2 x 0.1, which is 0.144 cubic metres. A 20 kg bag of dry mix yields roughly 0.009 cubic metres of placed concrete (about 9 litres), so the pad needs 0.144 divided by 0.009, which is 16 bags. Add about 10 percent for spillage and uneven ground and you buy 18 bags. If you would rather not do the volume maths with a pencil, a concrete bag calculator turns the dimensions straight into a bag count. At roughly 2.5 litres of water per bag, expect to use around 40 to 45 litres in total, added gradually across the batches rather than tipped in at once.

Frequently asked questions

How long do I have to place a hand mixed batch?

On a mild day, aim to place and finish each batch within about 30 to 45 minutes of the water going in. In hot or windy weather it stiffens faster, so mix smaller loads. Once concrete starts to stiffen, adding water to loosen it, called retempering, seriously weakens it, so discard material that has gone off rather than trying to rescue it.

Can I mix two bags at once in a standard barrow?

You can, but most people get a better, more even mix working one 20 to 25 kg bag at a time. A double load fills the barrow close to the rim, spills as you turn it, and is tiring to fold properly. Two smaller batches placed quickly beat one badly mixed big one.

Should I put washing up liquid in as a plasticiser?

No. Proper plasticiser is cheap and dosed for the job. Detergents drag in uncontrolled amounts of air, which can cut strength and leave the surface weak. If a mix feels harsh, a small dose of purpose made plasticiser, used exactly as the label directs, is the right answer.

Why did my concrete crack as it dried?

Fine surface cracks usually come from too much mixing water or from the surface drying out in sun and wind before it cured. Use the minimum water that makes the mix workable, shade fresh work on hot days, and keep it covered and damp for several days after placing.

Can I use sand from the beach or a bag of building sand?

Neither is a good choice. Beach sand carries salt and shells that corrode steel and stain the finish, and soft building sand is graded for mortar, not concrete. Buy washed sharp sand and clean coarse aggregate from a builders merchant.

How do I clean the barrow afterwards?

Rinse it out with water and a stiff brush as soon as the last batch leaves it, and never wash cement water into drains. A light smear of oil on the steel before you start makes the wash down far easier next time.

Conclusion

Good hand mixed concrete comes down to three habits: measure the dry ingredients honestly, blend them dry until the colour is even, and add water in small amounts until the mix just holds together. Keep batches small, place them promptly, and protect the finished work while it cures. Follow those rules and a wheelbarrow will turn out concrete every bit as reliable as the job demands.
